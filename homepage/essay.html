Write Your New York Essay

This essay centers around personal experience with protests held in the city and their impact on the community

The Ants

The first time I went to a protest was the first time I realized what New York City could be. I was seven years old, and that day I spent hours on my kitchen floor, pressing a Sharpie into a thin cardboard sign that read “Greed and hate are not human rights.” On the bus, I held it carefully tucked to my side, making sure the corners wouldn’t bend. It was a women’s march on a cold, gray morning, and thousands of us packed into the streets. The air buzzed with our voices spilling into one another until we became one continuous sound. I was young and small enough that I couldn’t see past the legs in front of me, but I didn’t need to. I could feel energy rushing through the sea of people.

When I got tired, my dad lifted me up onto his shoulders. Looking across the crowd, holding my sign up for the city to see, the sheer enormity of it was staggering. The streets shimmered with colorful signs catching in the pale sunlight, waves of motion that rolled through me as we raised our hands to the sky. At that moment, the city felt alive. We were beautiful and whole, tied together by something important.

I almost always feel the exact opposite. In New York, you get lost in the crowd instead of floating above it. People who once felt connected are now swarms of bent heads, like ants marching in lines, lives that slide past each other without touching. The sidewalks and subway cars are gray and crowded. The smells of petrol and hot dogs mingle with the sounds of late night car alarms and sirens. New York isn’t what they say it is: a unique gorgeous bustling place. In reality, New York is a collection of separate lives stacked side by side, sardines in a can. You can live your whole life in New York and never meet a fraction of its residents. Even those you manage to encounter, you don’t really know. Every morning you greet the same crossing guard, never asking her name. Her yellow vest is burned into your memory but if you saw her without it, you might not recognize her at all.

The design of the city sets us up for separation. Wide streets, delayed subways, and constant speed push us to keep moving. We don’t smile at the people across from us in our subway car, or nod at someone on the street, because we are going too quickly, and we always have somewhere to be. Slowly, the constant motion and vastness of the city teach us that we are anonymous and alone. The bustling streets are a reminder of just how small and insignificant each of us is in the grand scale of the city.

Protests are a unique New York experience because they reveal what the city hides from us. Most days, people live in isolation: commuters buried in their phones, pedestrians walking briskly along the sidewalk, neighbors never saying a word to one another. But in a protest, boundaries and separation dissolve. Complete strangers hand out snacks and water, cheer for each other, compliment signs, ask for photos, and exchange smiles. These acts of kindness accumulate in the streets, creating a sense of unity not often felt. We spot workers in suits walking alongside students, kids at their first protest, and people who have been to every one of these marches since they can remember. The hum of the crowd doesn’t silence individuals, it amplifies our collective voice. Usually, we struggle to connect, but here we are moving under a common goal, and instead of separate entities we become parts of a whole, ants working in a colony. Streets that foster separation become shared spaces alive with vibrancy and movement. In those moments, we experience the city differently. It turns from an isolating labyrinth into a place that holds power.

The city can make us feel small, but it can also uplift us. Its scale and constant speed lend itself to our collective power. We can transform streets into a place of action, energy, and connection rather than complacency and powerlessness. Protests show the city at its best, when we are not each in our own world. In these moments we find New Yorkers are powerful. New York can not only show us our power but the way we engage with the city shapes our experience of it. By choosing connection and community, we turn these streets into a setting for energy and possibility, so that when we allow it, New York becomes a place full of life, movement, and power.
